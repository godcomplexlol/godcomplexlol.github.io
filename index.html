A few frames of timer variation doesn’t prove lolmelody cut his footage

Written and built by 9kwr

A chart claims lolmelody’s run contains cuts because an on-screen reading lands 4 to 18 frames away from a whole number of seconds. I rebuilt the chart from its own timestamps. Here is what holds up, what doesn’t, and what would settle it.

What the check found

  • Two rows have arithmetic errors, and one timestamp is mistyped (“7:40.15”).
  • The chart reports variation without a sign. With signs, four of six gaps come out longer than a whole number of seconds, which small cuts can’t produce.
  • A frame-rate-dependent timer is a plausible alternative, but the chart can’t confirm it. It needs the FPS at each timestamp.
  • None of this proves he never cut. A cut leaves a visible or audible break at the timestamp.

How the chart works

Each row picks two moments where the same repeating event shows up, then checks how far the gap between them is from a whole number of seconds. Timestamps are minutes:seconds:frame at 60 frames per second. The method assumes the event repeats on an exact whole-second period, so a clean recording should give a variation of zero.

frame index  F = 3600·m + 60·s + f
gap  G = F_end − F_start
whole seconds  N = round(G / 60)
variation  v = G − 60·N   (negative = shorter, positive = longer)

The chart prints only |v|. The other four players land within 0 to 1 frame, even across gaps of 1,295 to 2,728 seconds. lolmelody lands 4 to 18 frames out on every row.

The two flagged rows don’t change any frame variation, so they don’t overturn the claim on their own. They do show the chart wasn’t checked carefully, which is a reason to ask for the source numbers.

Direction matters

Signed, the six values are −10, +4, +9, +18, −10 and +11 frames. Their mean is +3.7 frames with a standard deviation of 11.5, so the mean is 0.78 standard errors from zero. The 5% threshold for six samples is about 2.57, so the average offset isn’t distinguishable from none.

If frames were removed between two readings, the gap is G = 60N − c and v = −c, so small cuts (under half a second) always read negative. Four positive rows mean something other than small cuts is involved. One caveat: a cut larger than half a second wraps around. A 42-frame cut would print as +18. That is 0.7 seconds of missing gameplay, which would normally leave a visible jump, and this method can’t tell the two cases apart. It also can’t see cuts of an exact whole number of seconds.

Does it grow with the gap?

A constant clock difference would make v grow in proportion to the gap. Here the signed correlation is 0.77 (using |v| it is 0.57), and the fitted slope is about 0.12 frames per second. With six points, a correlation needs to reach about 0.81 to clear the 5% bar, so this is a hint of drift, not proof. Removing footage would push longer gaps more negative, and the trend runs the other way.

What could produce a 4 to 18 frame offset

  • Reading error. Picking each timestamp to within a frame gives at most ±2 frames on a gap. That rules out sloppy reading as the whole story, since the offsets run up to 18.
  • A small clock mismatch. 60 and 59.94 FPS differ by about one part in 1,000 [3]. That is 0.06 frames per second of gap: about 1.3 frames over 22 s and 12.4 over 206 s. It only applies if lolmelody’s recording or timer differs from the others, whose long gaps stay within one frame.
  • A timer tied to frame rate. Roblox’s default client cap is 60 FPS, and players can raise it to 240 on Windows [1], so a 165 cap is a personal setting. Frame rate also varies from moment to moment, and the engine’s work is done frame by frame, with some frames taking much longer than others [2]. If the on-screen reading refreshes on a frame-based schedule, its timing shifts when FPS shifts. I couldn’t find Roblox documentation of how often the counter refreshes, so this stays a hypothesis until the FPS values at each timestamp are shown.
  • Variable-rate recording. A video file can store frames at uneven intervals, and comparing ffprobe’s r_frame_rate with avg_frame_rate is the usual check [4]. Recording software can produce such files, and then timestamps read at a nominal 60 FPS drift.
  • Cuts. Possible, and nothing here excludes them. But the offsets alone aren’t evidence of them.

What would settle it

  • The frame at each flagged timestamp, looking for a jump, a repeated frame, or an audio break.
  • The FPS readout at both ends of every row, so the link between FPS and variation can be tested on all rows instead of picked from a few.
  • The same measurement on the other players’ runs, with their FPS.
  • ffprobe or MediaInfo output for lolmelody’s file, to rule variable frame rate in or out.
  • A fresh run with FPS held fixed. If the variation disappears, the timer explanation holds.

This page doesn’t prove he never cut. It shows that this chart doesn’t demonstrate it, and that the evidence offered has ordinary explanations that haven’t been ruled out.
